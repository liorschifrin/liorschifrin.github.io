Project 1.1: Nametag

After configuring Prusa Slicer for the Prusa Mini, I downloaded and added the nametag file to Pursa Slicer. I had a bit of trouble editing the text and moving them until I figured out I had to double click and click and drag directly on the text. I also added a url of liorschifrin.github.io to the nametag to see if I could add the website that I will soon be making to the nametag as well. As the text was very thin I assumed that it would likely not print correctly but I wanted to try anyway, to see where the limits are in terms of small text as well. I then sliced and exported the print to a USB drive without problem. I consulted the linked documentation on color changes in Prusa Slicer but did not need to change anything as the color change was in the file. Surprisingly, the print actually succeeded. It did have the problem of filament stringing across the letters. For the large letters they were still legible, and provided a spider web look. The small letters got a little messed up and harder to read. The other problem I ran into was in Prusa Slicer the dots disappeared when slicing in the url. So it just says liorschifrin github io instead of liorschifrin.github.io, but I think it still suits its purpose.

Project 1.2: Calibration Print

I selected the double helix print for this step. I was very unsure if this print would succeed, but there were photos of it printing on the site, so I imagined it must be possible. After printing I can confirm that it was in fact possible. I find this quite impressive. As the ladders are rotating, there is a small overhang every layer throughout the entire print. Since the overhang is so small, it is understandable how it succeeded. There also were bridges that were over an inch long that made the rungs of the ladder, and I'm quite impressed that PLA made its way across the entire rung without seriously drooping. The print did face the problem of being very stringy, which is a fairly serious aesthetic problem. I'm not sure if it's related to the print itself or to the printer settings. The fact that my previous print also was very stringy, and it was on the same printer with the same spool of filament, might suggest that something in print settings or printer settings could be affecting stringiness. There was also a long bridge to make so I wouldn't be surprised if attempting to make a long bridge created very thin stringy layers. To attempt to solve the stringing issues, I consulted this 3D Print quality troubleshooting guide which led me to retraction settings, and to this prusa help article for specific settings to change. The two adjustments I made are:

  • Enabling "Wipe on retraction" which will first move the nozzle back to "wipe" any lasting filament after retracting before a move
  • Changing retraction length from 2.5 to 3.2, which will affect how far the filament is retracted into the extruder
There are also many other things that could be affecting stringiness such as nozzle temperature. The prusa help article also mentioned that the filament could have gathered moisture, so I will be using a different filament as well. It certainly seems like there are limits to 3D printing, but I don't believe I hit them. If I tried again with different settings, I believe I could produce a better result.

STL is a file format from 1987 that just has the shape of the object. It does not include any information about scale, color, materials, or print settings. Conversely, 3MF was created in 2015 for additive manufacturing and contains all relevant information in a single file. This includes texture, color, material, manufacturing data like Prusa Slicer settings, as well as designer name, description, copyright and license info, and a thumbnail. This is especially useful for custom supports, infills, colors, and variable layer height. The data is also stored as human readable xml and is compressed, so is often 2-3 times smaller than a similar STL file. Source: blog.prusa3d.com

Project 1.3: Open-source Assistive Devices

I printed a one handed book holder, designed by Mathis and posted on MyMiniFactory. Found on the Makers Making Change website. The goal of the device is to make it easier to hold a book open with one hand. As said in the description, "[u]sers with limited hand dexterity or use of only one hand may find this device beneficial." It seemed to work fine. I would have to read with it for a longer period of time to see how effective it really is. Though, as I have a standard number of hands and hand dexterity I might not be able to judge how effective it would be for someone else. Documentation included a description and a recommendation for .2mm layer height and 10% infill. I don't think it needed anything further. I might suggest having a few different sized holes for different finger sizes for a better fit, though it should still work fine, it will just be a bit loose or tight depending on finger size.

Fusion Tutorial

The Fusion tutorial was much different from Tinkercad. I quickly learned that the simplest of things in Tinkercad was much more complicated in Fusion. To just create a 3D object you had to first create a 2D sketch of the object on the proper plane, then extrude it. Through the tutorial I learned this process well. There were many other features featured in the tutorial, you might say, but many of them I did not absorb in the process. There were some moments of frustration when something I was trying to do following the tutorial would be different or just wouldn’t work.

Remaking the Tinkercad object in Fusion was certainly the most frustrating experience of my semester so far. What had been so simple had become so complex. Many things did not go to plan. I had lots of trouble trying to recreate the top hexagon. I wasn't sure which plane to sketch it on, or if I needed to sketch both an outer and inner hexagon, or just one that I could use the shell tool on. I restarted twice in the process of remaking the design, and ended up creating most of the components individually, and then bringing them together in one design after. One problem I faced was that all the parts would fuse together and I couldn’t move or edit them individually after. I realized once finished that I could have created new components to separate them which would have helped I imagine.

I started by trying to match the design and measurements exactly, but by the 3rd attempt I just wanted something similar. It is certainly different from the original, but I called it to be close enough.

Project 3.1: Soldering and Design

I chose to create an alarm clock, as I thought I would be more likely to actually use it compared to a radio. I expected to face challenges on figuring out what parts go where and with the actual soldering itself. The “instruction manual” included instructions for how to operate the alarm clock and a very basic diagram and table of each component and where it goes. This proved surprisingly useful, as it took me about 20 minutes to line up each part in the kit to its name with some Googling and process of elimination, and after that all I had to do was put each part in and solder it. Including more details would certainly make it easier to identify the parts, but I was actually quite happy with the simplistic instructions.

The soldering actually went really well. I put all the pieces in and soldered them up and faced no problems. I did run into a scare later where the chip was smoking and I thought it was fried, but then it started working again and I’m not going to question it!


These are the measurements I took of the completed alarm clock. Some proved very useful and some were completely unnecessary, for the future I will focus on measuring the big picture elements. There were some additional measurements I had to take throughout, and I found a small ruler to be a good enough and easier measure than the digital calipers.

I added plenty of space on each side as I wasn’t sure how much extra I needed. The affordances I needed were: ability to touch the buttons, being able to see the screen and LEDs, and being able to access the battery connection. The affordances I aspirationally wanted to include if I had more time were a battery on/off switch without having to disconnect it, a cover for the speaker to make the alarm much quieter, and better buttons.

Project 3.2: 3D Printing an enclosure

I printed with normal settings wihtout hitches. Here are the four iterations I printed for determining proper dimensions. The first design was about 3mm too big on each side, due to the amount of space I added. I did note that the space for the screen was good, and liked the top design of one cutout for the screen and one cutout for the speaker and buttons.

The second iteration fixed the dimensions on the bottom and made the battery wires cutout smaller, although I made the walls less tall to save time and material so it was difficult to tell if it was exactly right as the walls weren’t taller than the solder on the bottom. I adjusted the dimensions for the top quickly to get a print completed before the end of class, so I miscalculated and made the thickness of the top bar too big.

For the third iteration, I made a version with taller walls to better see how it fit. I adjusted the dimensions for the top and was happy with how both of them turned out. The next thing to do was to connect the top and bottom, as they up until now were two different parts. I wanted to use a sliding dovetail top, so I prototyped just the sliding top following this tutorial with .25mm clearance before printing the whole thing

Then, I went for the final print including the sliding top.
I grew to really like Fusion over the course of this project. I learned a lot by designing something on my own, asking Avery a lot of questions, and following tutorials. Learning about parameters blew my mind, and making everything a variable made my CS-oriented brain very happy. I can see now the power of Fusion, and got very comfortable with making sketches and extruding with lines and rectangles using different constraints and was amazed with the way things just worked (when set up correctly).
